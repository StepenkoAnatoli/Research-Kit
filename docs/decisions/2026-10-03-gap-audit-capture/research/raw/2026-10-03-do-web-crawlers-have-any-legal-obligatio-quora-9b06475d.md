Web crawlers do not have a legal obligation to respect robots.txt.

Since web crawlers are simply programs for data discovery & collection, the creator of the web crawler can use robots.txt as a directive for crawling, but can also choose to ignore and/or not check for its presence entirely.

However, the users/creators of the web crawlers as well as the data they gather, must still follow copyright, data usage, and privacy policy guidelines.

In some regions/countries, legal teams do defer to robots.txt.

What Is Robots.txt?

Robots.txt is a file that tells search engine spiders to not crawl specific pages or sections of a website. Most important search engines including Google, Bing, and Yahoo recognize and honor Robots.txt requests.

![](https://qph.cf2.quoracdn.net/main-qimg-bcdf7f40183d58c5ad4181038419d9ff-pjlq)

Why Is Robots.txt Important?

Most websites don’t require a robots.txt file. That’s because Google can usually see and index all of the necessary pages on your site.

And they’ll automatically NOT index pages that aren’t important or duplicate versions of other pages.

That said, there are 3 main reasons that you’d want to use a robots.txt file.

1.Block Non-Public Pages: Sometimes you have pages on your site that you don’t want indexing. For example, you might have a staging version of a page. Or a login page. Those pages require to exist. But you don’t want random people landing on them. This is a case where you’d use robots.txt to block these pages from search engine crawlers and bots.

2.Maximize Crawl Budget: If you’re having a tough time making all of your pages indexed, you might have a crawl budget problem. By blocking unimportant pages with robots.txt, Googlebot can spend more of your crawl budget on the pages that matter.

3.Prevent Indexing of Resources: Applying meta directives can work just as well as Robots.txt for preventing pages from going indexed. However, meta directives don’t work well for multimedia resources, like PDFs and images. That’s where robots.txt comes into play.

The bottom line? Robots.txt tells search engine spiders not to crawl specific pages on your website.

How to find your robots.txt file

If you already have a robots.txt file on your website, it’ll be accessible. Navigate to the URL in your browser. If you see something like this, then you have a robots.txt file.

If you don’t already have a robots.txt file, creating one is easy. Just open a blank .txt document and begin typing directives.

For example, if you wanted to disallow all search engines from crawling your /admin/ directory, it would look something like this:

User-agent: \*

Disallow: /admin/

Continue to build up the directives until you’re happy with what you have. Save your file as “robots.txt.”

Robots.txt is a text (not html) file you put on your site to tell search robots which pages you would like them not to visit. Robots.txt is by no means mandatory for search engines but generally search engines obey what they are asked not to do. It is important to clarify that robots.txt is not a way from preventing search engines from crawling your site (i.e. it is not a firewall, or a kind of password protection) and the fact that you put a robots.txt file is something like putting a note “Please, do not enter” on an unlocked door.

When a robot looks for the "/robots.txt" file for URL, it strips the path component from the URL (everything from the first single slash), and puts "/robots.txt" in its place.

There are a few claims that the owner of a website can raise if a third party crawls his site. I group them into 2 groups: equitable claims and statutory claims.

I refer to one group as 'equitable' because they enable a court to issue an injunction to stop continued crawling. This is important, because it can effectively destroy a company if the company's business depends on crawling that particular site.

The equitable claims include breach of contract, trespass to chattels, and unjust enrichment. The statutory claims include the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), and violations of copyright law.

The equitable claims all require the plaintiff to prove two important things. 1) that the defendant accessed the plaintiff's physical website (not just the content by viewing the site through Google, etc.); and 2) that the defendant violated the terms of use of the site. The claims differ in damages.

Breach of contract and trespass to chattels return the plaintiff's lost profits if they directly resulted from the crawling. They also return the cost associated with repairing or replacing physical property that was damaged as a result of the crawling.

Unjust Enrichment gives the plaintiff the ability to claim profits earned by the defendant as a direct result of the crawling.

The statutory claims have sharper teeth. CFAA arguably makes web scraping a crime. Copyright does too (but in reality, the government does not prosecute copyright cases very frequently). You might go to jail for violating CFAA. But it's really unlikely.

CFAA requires that the defendant "access" a server and "exceed" authorization in doing so. Courts have pretty uninformally decided that violating a terms of use constitutes accessing a server in excess of authorization. However, courts differ in the standards they apply. For example, some courts require that a terms of use be on the front page of the site in order to be enforceable, some courts require that they be within at least 1 click of the landing page, etc.

Importantly, there seems to be an unsettling split wherein some courts think that a violation of CFAA can constitute a crime (punishable by imprisonment) and others don't.

Criminal Discussion of CFAA

Many courts have refused to apply CFAA's criminal provisions because, they say, those provisions are unconstitutional. The argument is, by rendering the violation of a terms of use provision a crime, we make terms of service provisions the law. This is unconstitutional, the courts say, because people do not have notice of the terms of use provisions in the same way that they do of the law. Terms of Use provisions change all the time - so allowing them to serve as law would violate the due process provision of the constitution.

This view of the law seems to be the prevailing view. But note, there have been some prosectutions under CFAA. Aarow Schwartz is the most notable. So, its not like there is no risk of criminal prosecution.

From a damages perspective, CFAA can be expensive. It entitles the plaintiff to the cost of adding more security to prevent future crawling. These costs can get pretty high.

Copyright law will apply with any scraping of blogs, or newsites, or other sites with original content. It will not apply if you are scraping addresses, or other data without some creative merit to it. Also, not all scraping of original content will constitute a copyright violation. I am not an expert in fair use, but borrowing a few lines in a quote, for example, is considered "fair use," generally.

ELEMENTS

Violating Terms of Use

As far as I know, a terms of use provision must affirmatively prohibit crawling in order for crawling to be considered a breach of the terms of use. That is to say, if a terms of use provision does not say that it limits access to bots, spyders, etc, and it does not prohibit rapid requests of the server, crawling is fine. Similarly, if the terms of use does not provide a limitation on a particular use of the data, anything goes so long as the crawler does not violate copyright.

Accessing Server

A person does not access a host server if he gets the host's data from another site (i.e. Google). So, if the crawler never pings the host's server/url, none of the above claims apply.

A webcrawler is a program that reads web pages, and usually follows links. Most of them do this to index the site on search engines, but there are other purposes, like automated price checking and similar.

They are legal. Some individual websites or specific pages might say ‘no crawlers allowed’ - there’s even a technical way to do this using Robots.txt or NOINDEX flags. Breaking this is not a crime, but might get a specific crawler banned by the site owners if it misbehaves.

For answering this question, I need to explain what is the purpose of a robots.txt file on any website.

In simple language:

For any website or any of its web pages to rank on Google, first, it has to be crawled by the search engine … this is done so that Google can understand the contents of your website or say web page.

This will help Google show your website for the right search queries typed on the search bar.

Now,

The robots.txt file is added for preventing certain or selective pages on your website from being crawled by Google.

This is done because you don’t want those web pages to be indexing or showing up in the search rankings.

But why will you want that?

Why will anyone ever want something like that?

Let’s go through some examples:

1. Imagine a banking website,

it will have the private details of its customers like their account number, PIN, mobile number, etc.

This data is too sensitive to fall in the wrong hands or to be made public.

So what the banking websites do is that they’ll add their webpages containing that data into the robots.txt file. That way Google will not be able to crawl those web pages & thus they’ll never rank i.e your information is safe.

.

Let's take another example:

Imagine that your website has some faulty web pages,

- the content on them is below average
- contains broken links
- the layout is also unimpressive

.

Now the problem is that your website’s ranking will be affected by the quality of ALL its web pages (including the faulty ones)

For ensuring that while you’re working towards fixing those pages your website’s rankings aren’t affected what you can do is …

put those web pages into a robots.txt file.

That way you …

- don’t have to create new web pages from scratch
- Your website will continue to rank
- You can make the changes at your pace & update them on your website

.

You’re getting it right?

Awesome 😃

.

Now,

coming to your question -

Is It Necessary To Have Robots.txt file on your website?

Straight-cut answer NO.

If you’re website is working fine & you don’t have ANY faulty web pages then it doesn’t make sense to add a robots file, but …

if you’re website has certain issues (could be anything) due to which you don’t want those to get indexed, … you already know what needs to be done.

.

If you’re making your website on Wordpress then it will not take more than 5 minutes, in the backend you’ll get tools for helping you upload your txt files in a single click.

So its important to keep this learning in your mind EVEN if everything seems to be going good, because you never know what emergency might creep in.
