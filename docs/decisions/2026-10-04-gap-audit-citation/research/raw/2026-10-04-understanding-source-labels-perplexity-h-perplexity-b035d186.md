When Perplexity cites a source, some citations carry a small shield icon. The icon marks a domain that Perplexity’s source review process has rated, and it comes with one of three labels: **Government**, **Academic**, or **Trusted**. Select or hover over a source to see its label and a short explanation of why the domain received it. Labels help you see at a glance what kind of source you are reading.

## [​](https://www.perplexity.ai/help-center/en/articles/20260806-understanding-source-labels\#h_how)  **How Perplexity labels sources**

Perplexity rates the whole website a source comes from, not each individual page. The source review process decides whether a site should carry a label by checking objective questions, such as:

- Does the site correct its mistakes?

- Does it say who wrote each piece?

- Does it keep news separate from advertising and opinion?


Sites that clearly meet these tests receive a label. Labels are set by the source review process alone. Perplexity’s partnerships, payments, and other business arrangements do not affect a site’s label.

Most domains on the web do not have a label. No label does not mean a site is low quality, and it is not a negative judgment. It means the domain has not been rated yet, or there is not yet a clear rating for it. A label is also not an endorsement of what a site publishes.

## [​](https://www.perplexity.ai/help-center/en/articles/20260806-understanding-source-labels\#h_labels)  **The three labels**

Rated sources fall into one of three categories:

- **Government** — the official website of a government organization. This label helps with legal questions, financial matters, government services, and similar topics. Examples: `nasa.gov` (NASA missions, space and Earth science, aeronautics research, and official data and education resources from the U.S. civil space agency) and `epa.gov` (U.S. federal environmental regulation, science-based pollution control guidance, and agency data and program information).

- **Academic** — scientific sites. This label helps with medicine, biology, physics, mathematics, and similar topics. Examples: `science.org` (peer-reviewed scientific research, news, and careers information published by AAAS’s _Science_ journals) and `journals.plos.org` (open-access peer-reviewed scientific and medical journal articles from the nonprofit publisher PLOS).

- **Trusted** — a broad category for sites that appear often enough in results to receive a label and that publish information within their own area of expertise. Examples: `reuters.com` (international news wire covering global business, politics, and world events) and `mayoclinic.org` (patient health information published by the Mayo Clinic).

## [​](https://www.perplexity.ai/help-center/en/articles/20260806-understanding-source-labels\#h_reading)  **Reading a label**

Select or hover over a source in an answer to open its details. The panel shows the source’s label along with a one-sentence description of what the site is known for — for example, “nasa.gov is a government source for NASA missions, space and Earth science, aeronautics, official data, news, and education.” That context helps you judge the source at a glance.

Each source panel also includes **Rate this source**, which you can use to send feedback about that domain.

## [​](https://www.perplexity.ai/help-center/en/articles/20260806-understanding-source-labels\#h_limits)  **What labels do not tell you**

A label describes the website as a whole, not any single article or claim on it. It is not an opinion about the site’s views. A label can change as sites are reviewed again. And a label is never a substitute for reading the source yourself.

## [​](https://www.perplexity.ai/help-center/en/articles/20260806-understanding-source-labels\#h_good)  **Good to know**

- Labels reflect the type or category of an overall website, not the accuracy of any single article or claim on it.

- A label does not mean Perplexity agrees with a site’s views, and the absence of a label is not a judgment that a source is low quality.

- Labels can change over time as sites are reassessed.

- Perplexity aims for accuracy, but reviewing the original sources yourself remains the best way to build confidence in an answer.
